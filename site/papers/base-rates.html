Why does a 99% accurate test get it wrong most of the time: the research

Base rates, predictive value, and why test accuracy is routinely misread.

August 2026 · about 7 minutes · the long layer behind the story

Abstract

We examined why positive predictive value is so widely misunderstood. The arithmetic is elementary and the intuition is reliably wrong, including among professionals, which makes it one of the highest-value things a general reader can learn.

What we looked at

We examined why positive predictive value is so widely misunderstood. The arithmetic is elementary and the intuition is reliably wrong, including among professionals, which makes it one of the highest-value things a general reader can learn.

Findings

Each one carries the confidence we are willing to defend it at. We use three levels only; more would be false precision.

  1. 1

    Positive predictive value depends on prevalence.

    Well established

    Elementary probability.

  2. 2

    Clinicians frequently misestimate it.

    Well established

    Replicated repeatedly since the 1970s.

  3. 3

    Presenting figures as natural frequencies improves accuracy.

    Well established

    Gigerenzer and colleagues; large effect.

  4. 4

    Screening rare conditions produces mostly false positives.

    Well established

    Direct consequence.

What is contested

Live disagreements, stated as disagreements rather than resolved for tidiness.

How much population screening does net harm.

Depends entirely on condition, test and follow-up burden; the debate over some cancer screening programmes is exactly this argument.

What we could not establish

  • Why frequency framing helps so much more than probability framing.

Method, and its limits

Worked from first principles and checked against the published misestimation literature.

Further reading

Described so you know what each one is for, rather than listed to look thorough.

  1. Gigerenzer, Reckoning with Risk

    The clearest treatment, and the source of the frequency-framing fix.

  2. Casscells et al. (1978)

    The original demonstration that clinicians get this wrong.
