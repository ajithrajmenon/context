What is it like to fall into a black hole: the research

Event horizons, proper time, and the limits of general relativity.

August 2026 · about 7 minutes · the long layer behind the story

Abstract

We set out what an infalling observer experiences versus a distant one, and identified where general relativity ceases to give defined answers.

What we looked at

We set out what an infalling observer experiences versus a distant one, and identified where general relativity ceases to give defined answers.

Findings

Each one carries the confidence we are willing to defend it at. We use three levels only; more would be false precision.

  1. 1

    An infalling observer detects nothing locally at the horizon.

    Well established

    Follows from the equivalence principle for large black holes.

  2. 2

    A distant observer sees infall asymptotically slow and redshift.

    Well established

    Standard result.

  3. 3

    Tidal forces at the horizon decrease with black hole mass.

    Well established

    Direct from the metric.

  4. 4

    General relativity predicts a singularity of infinite curvature.

    Well established

    And is generally read as the theory failing there.

What is contested

Live disagreements, stated as disagreements rather than resolved for tidiness.

What the information paradox implies about the horizon.

Proposals range from firewalls to holographic encoding. No consensus, and the disagreement is about whether the horizon really is featureless.

What we could not establish

  • What replaces the singularity in a quantum theory of gravity.
  • Whether information escaping in Hawking radiation is recoverable in practice.

Method, and its limits

Standard general relativity results. Where the theory produces infinities we report that as a limit of the theory rather than a physical fact.
