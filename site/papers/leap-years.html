Why do we have leap years: the research

Calendar construction and the reconciliation of the tropical year with the solar day.

August 2026 · about 7 minutes · the long layer behind the story

Abstract

We examined the arithmetic of the Gregorian calendar and its residual error, and the separate problem of variation in Earth's rotation.

What we looked at

We examined the arithmetic of the Gregorian calendar and its residual error, and the separate problem of variation in Earth's rotation.

Findings

Each one carries the confidence we are willing to defend it at. We use three levels only; more would be false precision.

  1. 1

    The tropical year is approximately 365.2422 days.

    Well established

    Precisely measured.

  2. 2

    The Julian calendar overcorrects by about three days per four centuries.

    Well established

    Direct arithmetic.

  3. 3

    The Gregorian rule leaves an error of roughly one day per 3,200 years.

    Well established

    Direct arithmetic.

  4. 4

    Earth's rotation rate varies unpredictably year to year.

    Well established

    Continuously monitored.

What is contested

Live disagreements, stated as disagreements rather than resolved for tidiness.

Whether leap seconds should have been abolished.

The 2022 decision to phase them out by 2035 was contested by parts of the astronomy community, who use the tie to Earth's rotation.

What we could not establish

  • How rotational variation will behave over the coming centuries.
  • What will replace leap seconds once drift becomes noticeable.

Method, and its limits

Arithmetic worked directly from published values for the tropical year and mean solar day.
