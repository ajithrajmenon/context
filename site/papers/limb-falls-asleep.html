What is happening when a limb falls asleep: the research

The mechanism of transient compression neuropathy, and how to distinguish it from persistent nerve injury.

August 2026 · about 7 minutes · the long layer behind the story

Abstract

We set out to check the popular circulation explanation for paraesthesia. It is largely incorrect. The sensation is produced by mechanical compression of nerve fibres and their uneven recovery, which matters because it changes which symptoms should prompt concern.

What we looked at

We set out to check the popular circulation explanation for paraesthesia. It is largely incorrect. The sensation is produced by mechanical compression of nerve fibres and their uneven recovery, which matters because it changes which symptoms should prompt concern.

Findings

Each one carries the confidence we are willing to defend it at. We use three levels only; more would be false precision.

  1. 1

    Transient paraesthesia is caused by nerve compression, not ischaemia alone.

    Well established

    Ischaemia contributes; the sensation tracks nerve conduction.

  2. 2

    Pins and needles occur during recovery, not compression.

    Well established

    Consistent with the reported time course.

  3. 3

    Repeated or sustained compression can cause lasting nerve damage.

    Well established

    The basis of carpal tunnel and related entrapments.

  4. 4

    Sensory fibres are affected before motor fibres.

    Well established

    Explains numbness preceding weakness.

What is contested

Live disagreements, stated as disagreements rather than resolved for tidiness.

How much ischaemia contributes alongside mechanical deformation.

Both occur together in ordinary positional compression and are difficult to separate experimentally in humans.

What we could not establish

  • Why susceptibility to positional compression varies so much between people.
  • The threshold of duration at which occasional compression starts to cause lasting change.

Method, and its limits

Synthesis of neurology reference material on compression neuropathies. Clinical entrapment literature was treated as the strongest evidence, since it is operational.
