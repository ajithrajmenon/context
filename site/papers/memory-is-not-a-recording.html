Why are confident memories so often wrong: the research

Reconstructive memory, reconsolidation, and the confidence-accuracy relationship.

August 2026 · about 7 minutes · the long layer behind the story

Abstract

We examined whether the reconstructive account of memory is as strong as popular writing suggests. It is, and if anything is understated. The confidence-accuracy dissociation is well established and has direct legal consequences.

What we looked at

We examined whether the reconstructive account of memory is as strong as popular writing suggests. It is, and if anything is understated. The confidence-accuracy dissociation is well established and has direct legal consequences.

Findings

Each one carries the confidence we are willing to defend it at. We use three levels only; more would be false precision.

  1. 1

    Recall is reconstructive rather than reproductive.

    Well established

    A century of converging evidence.

  2. 2

    Retrieval can alter the stored memory.

    Well established

    Reconsolidation, robust in animals and supported in humans.

  3. 3

    Confidence is a poor predictor of accuracy for detailed recall.

    Well established

    Especially after delay and repeated questioning.

  4. 4

    Flashbulb memories are vivid but not more accurate.

    Well established

    Longitudinal studies after major events.

What is contested

Live disagreements, stated as disagreements rather than resolved for tidiness.

Can false memories be reliably distinguished from true ones?

No marker has held up. Neither confidence, detail, nor vividness separates them reliably, which is the uncomfortable finding.

What we could not establish

  • How much of everyday autobiographical memory is confabulated.
  • Whether reconsolidation can be used therapeutically at scale.

Method, and its limits

Synthesis of memory research and forensic psychology. We weighted longitudinal studies with contemporaneous records above laboratory analogues.
