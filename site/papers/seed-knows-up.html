How does a seed know which way is up: the research

Gravitropism: the starch-statolith mechanism and auxin redistribution.

August 2026 · about 7 minutes · the long layer behind the story

Abstract

We examined how plants detect gravity. The starch-statolith hypothesis with auxin-mediated differential growth is well supported, including by microgravity experiments and starch-deficient mutants.

What we looked at

We examined how plants detect gravity. The starch-statolith hypothesis with auxin-mediated differential growth is well supported, including by microgravity experiments and starch-deficient mutants.

Findings

Each one carries the confidence we are willing to defend it at. We use three levels only; more would be false precision.

  1. 1

    Root cap cells contain dense, sedimenting starch-filled plastids.

    Well established

    Directly observed.

  2. 2

    Starch-deficient mutants show impaired gravitropism.

    Well established

    Key genetic evidence.

  3. 3

    Auxin redistributes to the lower flank after reorientation.

    Well established

    Measured with reporter lines.

  4. 4

    Auxin inhibits elongation in roots and promotes it in shoots.

    Well established

    Concentration-dependent; long characterised.

What is contested

Live disagreements, stated as disagreements rather than resolved for tidiness.

Whether statolith sedimentation is the only gravity sensor.

Some response persists in starch-deficient plants, so a secondary mechanism — possibly whole-protoplast pressure — likely exists.

What we could not establish

  • How the settled position is converted into a molecular signal.
  • How plants combine gravity with moisture and touch cues when they conflict.

Method, and its limits

Synthesis of plant physiology research, weighted toward mutant and microgravity evidence because both remove the variable directly.
