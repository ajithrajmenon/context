Why do you get a stitch when you run: the research

Exercise-related transient abdominal pain: mechanism, prevalence and prevention.

August 2026 · about 7 minutes · the long layer behind the story

Abstract

We reviewed the competing explanations for the exercise stitch. The diaphragmatic ischaemia model, still the most widely repeated, is poorly supported. Parietal peritoneal irritation currently explains the most features, but no mechanism is established.

What we looked at

We reviewed the competing explanations for the exercise stitch. The diaphragmatic ischaemia model, still the most widely repeated, is poorly supported. Parietal peritoneal irritation currently explains the most features, but no mechanism is established.

Findings

Each one carries the confidence we are willing to defend it at. We use three levels only; more would be false precision.

  1. 1

    Stitches occur in sports with no running gait.

    Well established

    Swimming and horse riding have high reported rates.

  2. 2

    Large meals and hypertonic drinks before exercise raise risk.

    Well established

    Consistent across surveys and trials.

  3. 3

    Diaphragmatic ischaemia is unlikely to be the cause.

    Best current explanation

    Fails to predict the observed pattern.

  4. 4

    Peritoneal irritation fits the evidence best.

    Contested

    Fits, but has not been shown directly.

What is contested

Live disagreements, stated as disagreements rather than resolved for tidiness.

What the actual tissue mechanism is.

No imaging study has caught a stitch in progress. Every mechanism on the table is inferred from symptom patterns rather than observed.

What we could not establish

  • Why some people get them constantly and others never.
  • Whether trunk training genuinely reduces incidence or just correlates with fitness.

Method, and its limits

Synthesis of sports medicine reviews. Where an explanation is inferred from symptom pattern rather than observed directly, we have said so instead of picking a winner.

Further reading

Described so you know what each one is for, rather than listed to look thorough.

  1. Morton and Callister, reviews of exercise-related transient abdominal pain

    The most thorough work on the question and honest about what is unresolved.
