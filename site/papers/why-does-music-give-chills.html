Why does music give you chills: the research

Musical frisson: its triggers, its neural correlates, and its habituation.

August 2026 · about 7 minutes · the long layer behind the story

Abstract

We examined what predicts the chill response to music. Expectation violation predicts it better than any acoustic property. The dopaminergic account is supported but the causal claim is stronger in popular writing than in the literature.

What we looked at

We examined what predicts the chill response to music. Expectation violation predicts it better than any acoustic property. The dopaminergic account is supported but the causal claim is stronger in popular writing than in the literature.

Findings

Each one carries the confidence we are willing to defend it at. We use three levels only; more would be false precision.

  1. 1

    Chills cluster at points of expectation violation.

    Well established

    Consistent across corpus and laboratory studies.

  2. 2

    Roughly half of listeners report chills reliably.

    Well established

    Individual variation is large and stable.

  3. 3

    Openness to experience predicts susceptibility.

    Well established

    One of the more reliable personality-perception links.

  4. 4

    Dopamine release accompanies peak musical pleasure.

    Best current explanation

    Imaging evidence; causal role less certain.

What is contested

Live disagreements, stated as disagreements rather than resolved for tidiness.

Is the response specific to music?

Similar chills occur with speech, film and physical touch. Whether the mechanism is shared is unresolved.

What we could not establish

  • Why a substantial minority never experience musical chills.
  • Whether the response can be induced in non-responders.

Method, and its limits

Synthesis of music cognition research. We treated within-subject designs as the strongest evidence, since between-subject variation is very large.

Further reading

Described so you know what each one is for, rather than listed to look thorough.

  1. Huron, Sweet Anticipation (2006)

    The expectation account, worked out properly.

  2. Salimpoor et al. (2011), Nature Neuroscience

    The imaging study behind the dopamine claim. Note what it does and does not show.
