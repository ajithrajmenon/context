Why does stress make you ill: the research

The physiology of acute and chronic stress, and which downstream harms are well supported.

August 2026 · about 7 minutes · the long layer behind the story

Abstract

We set out to separate well-supported physiology from the very large popular literature on stress. The acute cascade is thoroughly characterised. The chronic harms are real but the effect sizes are frequently overstated, and causal direction is often assumed rather than shown.

What we looked at

We set out to separate well-supported physiology from the very large popular literature on stress. The acute cascade is thoroughly characterised. The chronic harms are real but the effect sizes are frequently overstated, and causal direction is often assumed rather than shown.

Findings

Each one carries the confidence we are willing to defend it at. We use three levels only; more would be false precision.

  1. 1

    The acute cascade postpones immune, digestive and repair functions.

    Well established

    Basic and uncontroversial physiology.

  2. 2

    Chronic activation flattens the daily cortisol rhythm.

    Well established

    Measurable and consistent.

  3. 3

    Chronic stress increases susceptibility to infection.

    Best current explanation

    Experimental exposure studies support it; effect sizes are modest.

  4. 4

    Stress causes stomach ulcers.

    Contested

    Largely superseded. Most ulcers are H. pylori or NSAIDs; stress may modulate.

What is contested

Live disagreements, stated as disagreements rather than resolved for tidiness.

How much cardiovascular risk is causally attributable to stress?

Associations are strong and confounding is severe. People under chronic stress differ in sleep, diet, smoking and income, all of which carry their own risk.

What we could not establish

  • Why individuals vary so widely in stress reactivity.
  • Whether interventions that lower reported stress lower physiological markers durably.

Method, and its limits

Synthesis of endocrinology and psychoneuroimmunology reviews. We treated any claim resting only on self-reported stress as weak evidence.

Further reading

Described so you know what each one is for, rather than listed to look thorough.

  1. Sapolsky, Why Zebras Don't Get Ulcers

    The standard popular account, and unusually careful about what is not known.

  2. Cohen et al., on stress and experimental viral exposure

    Among the few designs that can speak to causation.
