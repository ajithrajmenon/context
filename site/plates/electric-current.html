Plate 10  /  Electromagnetism

Electrons crawl through a wire slower than you walk

In a one-millimetre copper wire carrying one amp, an individual electron drifts about seven hundredths of a millimetre per second. It would need most of a working day to cross a metre. The lamp still lights instantly, and the reason is not the electrons.

The usual picture
Electricity is electrons flying down the wire. Close the switch and they race to the bulb at nearly the speed of light, which is why it lights immediately.
What is actually happening
Electrons are already everywhere in the wire, jittering violently and drifting almost imperceptibly. What travels near light speed is the electromagnetic field, which tells every electron along the wire to start moving at once.

The failure

The timing does not work either way

If light-speed electrons were the mechanism, a wire would be a tube of near-empty space with particles shooting along it. But metal is packed with free electrons at roughly 1029 per cubic metre — about one per atom. There is no room to race.

And the racing model has a second problem: it predicts that a longer wire should light a lamp later in proportion to its length divided by electron speed. Switch it on above and watch the lamp wait. Nobody has ever observed that delay.

The mechanism

Jitter fast, drift slow, signal instantly

Three quite different speeds live in the same wire, and conflating them is the whole confusion.

  • Thermal jitter — around 106 m/s, random in every direction, contributing nothing net.
  • Drift — a tiny bias superimposed on that jitter, of order 0.1 mm/s. This is the current.
  • Field propagation — roughly two-thirds of light speed, guided by the wire, arriving essentially at once.

Close the switch and the field sweeps down the line, nudging electrons that were already sitting next to the lamp into motion. The bulb does not wait for anything to arrive from the switch.

The correction

The water pipe was right after all

The old plumbing analogy is much better than it gets credit for. Open a tap on a full pipe and water comes out immediately — not because molecules sprinted from the reservoir, but because the pipe was already full and pressure travels at the speed of sound in water.

Swap pressure for voltage and the analogy holds. The wire is already full of charge. The switch does not supply electrons; it establishes a field. Energy travels in the field around the wire, not in the electrons inside it.

Loose end

This is why alternating current works at all. At 50 Hz an electron oscillates back and forth over a fraction of a millimetre and ends each cycle where it started, having travelled nowhere net — and still delivers every watt in your house.
