Exponential growth looks like nothing until it looks like everything

The human default is to extrapolate in straight lines. Applied to anything that doubles — infections, compound interest, algal blooms — that instinct does not slightly underestimate the outcome. It misses it by orders of magnitude.

The usual picture
It has taken thirty days to reach 1% of capacity, so there is plenty of time. At this rate we will be fine for years.
What is actually happening
A quantity with a fixed doubling time spends most of its history looking negligible. Getting from 1% to 100% takes only about seven doublings — the same effort that took it from 0.008% to 1%.

The failure

Linear extrapolation is not conservative

The straight-line forecast is not a cautious estimate that errs on the safe side. Standing at 1.6% of capacity it puts the deadline decades out. The true answer is six days.

This is why exponential threats are chronically under-reacted to. At the moment when action is cheapest, the quantity is genuinely tiny — and the evidence of urgency looks identical to the evidence of nothing happening.

The mechanism

Constant ratio, not constant increment

Linear growth adds the same amount each step. Exponential growth multiplies by the same factor each step. The difference is not one of degree.

The consequence people miss: on a doubling curve, the amount added in the final step exceeds everything accumulated in all previous steps combined. The day before the pond is full, it is half empty. The day before that, a quarter.

And the fraction of the timeline spent looking negligible is enormous. Reaching 1% takes as many doublings from the start as reaching 100% takes from 1%.

The correction

Learn to read the doubling time

The one number that makes an exponential legible is not the current value or the current rate. It is the doubling time — and its arithmetic is easy. Divide 70 by the percentage growth per period and you get the doublings.

7% a year doubles in ten years, so it is 128× in a lifetime. 0.5% a week doubles in about 140 weeks. Once you have that number, "how long until this is a hundred times bigger" becomes seven doublings, and you can answer it in your head.

Loose end

Nothing grows exponentially forever — something always runs out, and the curve turns over into an S. But the moment of turnover is usually set by a hard limit arriving, not by the growth politely slowing. Assuming an S-curve does not license assuming a gentle one.
