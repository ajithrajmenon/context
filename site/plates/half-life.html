Plate 05  /  Nuclear physics

Atoms do not get old before they decay

A carbon-14 atom that has already survived ten thousand years is exactly as likely to decay in the next minute as one created a second ago. Nothing about it has changed. There is no clock inside it, and no wearing out.

The usual picture
Each atom has a lifespan. It sits there ageing, and when its time is up it decays. Half-life is the average age at which they wear out.
What is actually happening
Every intact atom has the same fixed probability of decaying in the next instant, forever. The process is memoryless. Half-life is not an age — it is the interval over which any given atom has a 50% chance of going.

The failure

Ageing predicts a cliff

If atoms wore out, a fresh sample would sit almost untouched for a while and then collapse together as they all reached their lifespan. Radiation output would be flat, then plunge.

Nothing like this has ever been observed. From the first measurements of radium onward, activity falls smoothly from the first instant — the signature of a constant per-atom probability, not a shared expiry date.

The mechanism

Memorylessness

Decay is a quantum tunnelling event. The nucleus is a particle behind an energy barrier it does not classically have the energy to cross, with some fixed probability per unit time of appearing on the other side.

Crucially, the nucleus retains no record of how long it has been waiting. Its state is identical today and a millennium hence. Survival confers no information about what comes next — the defining property of an exponential process.

So half-life is a statement about a population, not an individual. Among any large group, half will be gone after one half-life. Which half is unknowable in principle, not merely in practice.

The correction

The counter-intuitive consequence

Take a carbon-14 atom that has already sat intact for 5,730 years — one full half-life. What is its expected remaining lifetime?

The same as a brand new one. It has not used anything up. Human intuition resists this because everything in our experience wears out, but nuclei are not machines with moving parts.

The nearest everyday analogue is a fair coin. Ten tails in a row does not make heads overdue. The coin remembers nothing, and neither does the nucleus.

Loose end

This is also why radiometric dating works so cleanly. Because the probability is fixed and unaffected by temperature, pressure or chemistry, the ratio of parent to daughter atoms in a rock is a clock that nothing in ordinary geology can speed up or slow down.
