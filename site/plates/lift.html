Plate 04  /  Fluid dynamics

Air does not have to meet up at the back of the wing

This is the explanation in most textbooks, and it is false. The parcels never rejoin. The air over the top arrives at the trailing edge well ahead of the air underneath — and if it only travelled as fast as the story requires, aircraft could not fly.

The usual picture
The upper surface is longer, so air going over must speed up to meet its partner at the trailing edge. Faster air is lower pressure. That pressure difference is lift.
What is actually happening
The wing turns a large mass of air downward. Circulation around the aerofoil makes the upper flow far faster than any transit-time argument allows, and the reaction to all that downwash is lift.

The failure

The arithmetic does not close

Measure a typical airliner wing section and the upper surface is only a few percent longer than the lower. Equal transit time therefore demands the upper flow be about 3% faster. Feed that into Bernoulli and you get a pressure difference capable of supporting roughly a twentieth of the aircraft's weight.

Worse, the story cannot survive two obvious cases. A flat plate has identical path lengths top and bottom and still generates lift. And aircraft fly inverted, where the argument predicts they should be pressed harder into the ground.

The mechanism

Push air down, go up

Smoke-tunnel footage settles the transit question directly: the upper parcel reaches the trailing edge far ahead of its partner and they never rejoin. There was never a rule requiring them to.

What the wing does is establish circulation — a net rotational tendency in the flow around the aerofoil, set up as the wing starts moving and fixed by the condition that flow must leave the sharp trailing edge smoothly. Add circulation to the oncoming stream and the top speeds up while the bottom slows.

The consequence you can see is downwash. A wing in cruise deflects several tonnes of air downward every second. Newton's third law does the rest.

The correction

Bernoulli is not wrong, the story is

Faster air over the top really does mean lower pressure there, and integrating pressure around the aerofoil really does give you the lift. Bernoulli's principle is fine.

The error is the reason offered for why the air speeds up. Not because it has an appointment to keep at the trailing edge — because of circulation. Pressure and momentum-deflection are two accountings of one event, and they give the same number.

Loose end

Angle of attack matters more than aerofoil shape, which is why a barn door flies given enough speed. Push past about 15° and the flow separates from the upper surface, the circulation collapses, and the wing stalls — visible as the lift readout falling off a cliff at the top of the slider.
