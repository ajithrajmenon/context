Summer is not when the Earth is closer to the sun

Earth is nearest the sun in early January. If distance drove the seasons, January would be the hottest month of the year — and both hemispheres would have summer at the same time.

The usual picture
Earth's orbit is an ellipse. When we swing closer to the sun we get more heat, and that is summer.
What is actually happening
The axis is tilted 23.4° and always points the same way. That changes the angle sunlight strikes the ground, spreading the same beam over more or less area — and changes how long the sun stays up.

The failure

Run the numbers on distance

Switch to the distance model and drag through the year. Earth's orbit is very nearly circular — it ranges from 0.983 to 1.017 astronomical units. That is a 3.4% swing in distance and roughly 7% in received energy.

And the peak lands on 3 January. The model does not merely predict too small an effect. It predicts the seasons backwards for the half of humanity that lives north of the equator.

The mechanism

The same beam, spread thinner

Sunlight arrives as a beam of fixed intensity. What changes through the year is the angle it meets the ground.

Strike a surface head-on and a beam one metre wide covers one metre of ground. Strike it at 20° and the same beam smears across nearly three metres. Same energy, triple the area, a third of the heating. That is the whole of it.

Tilt does a second job as well: it lengthens the day. In June the northern hemisphere is turned toward the sun for more of each rotation, so the ground gets weaker-but-longer heating on top of stronger noon sun. The two effects compound.

The correction

Two hemispheres, opposite seasons, same instant

The decisive test costs nothing. It is July in Kerala and July in Melbourne at the same moment, and one is in summer while the other is in winter. Distance from the sun is identical for both. Tilt is not — the axis leans toward one hemisphere and away from the other, always.

The axis keeps pointing at the same patch of sky all year, at Polaris. It is the orbit that carries us around it, so the same fixed tilt faces the sun in June and away from it in December.

Loose end

The hottest weeks lag the solstice by about six weeks, because land and ocean take time to warm. Peak sunlight is late June; peak temperature is early August. Seasonal lag is why "longest day" and "hottest day" are never the same day.
