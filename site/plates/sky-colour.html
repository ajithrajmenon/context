Plate 03  /  Optics

The sunset is red for the same reason the sky is blue

Clean air over the Pacific produces sunsets just as red as any city. The blue overhead and the red on the horizon are two readings of a single effect, separated only by how much air the light had to cross.

The usual picture
Blue sky is air's own colour, or the sea's reflection. Red sunsets come from dust, smoke and pollution scattering the light.
What is actually happening
Air molecules scatter short wavelengths far more strongly than long ones — by a factor of 1/λ4. Overhead you see the scattered blue. At the horizon you look through 38× more air, and the blue has been scattered out of the beam before it reaches you.

The failure

Dust dims, it does not redden

Large particles — dust, smoke, water droplets — scatter all visible wavelengths at roughly the same rate. That is precisely why clouds and fog are white or grey rather than coloured.

Switch to the dust model and drop the sun. The disc gets darker and stays neutral. To produce red you need scattering that is selective by wavelength, and dust is the wrong size to be selective.

The mechanism

Why short wavelengths lose

Air molecules are far smaller than a wavelength of light. In that regime, scattering strength rises as the fourth power of frequency. Violet light at 400 nm scatters about nine times more than red at 700 nm.

Look away from the sun and you are seeing only light that got redirected into your eye — disproportionately the blue. Look straight at a low sun and you are seeing what survived the trip — disproportionately the red. One mechanism, two views.

The sky is not violet, despite violet scattering hardest, because sunlight contains less violet to begin with and our cones are far less sensitive to it.

The correction

Geometry, not chemistry

The variable that changes between noon and sunset is not what is in the air. It is how much air. Straight overhead you look through one air mass. At the horizon, refraction and curvature stretch the same atmosphere to roughly thirty-eight.

Pollution and volcanic aerosol do modify sunsets — sometimes spectacularly. But they are the garnish. Take them all away and the sunset stays red.

Loose end

Mars runs the effect in reverse. Its dust is the right size to scatter red forward, so the Martian sky is butterscotch by day and the sunsets are blue. Same physics, different particle size.
