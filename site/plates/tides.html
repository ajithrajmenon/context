Plate 01  /  Gravitation

The moon does not pull the sea toward it

If the moon simply pulled the water toward itself, coasts would get one high tide a day. They get two, roughly twelve hours apart — including one when the moon is on the far side of the planet.

The usual picture
The moon's gravity lifts the ocean into a single bulge pointing at the moon. One bulge sweeps past you once per rotation, so one high tide a day.
What is actually happening
The moon pulls the near water, the whole Earth, and the far water by different amounts. That difference stretches the ocean along the moon-line — a bulge at each end. You pass through two.

The failure

Why one bulge cannot be right

Set the switch to the one-bulge model and read the trace. It predicts one high tide every twenty-four hours. Every tide table on Earth disagrees: coasts see two highs and two lows in just under twenty-five hours.

The model is not slightly off. It is wrong by a whole peak.

The mechanism

Gravity that varies across a body

Gravity weakens with distance. The ocean facing the moon is about 12,700 km closer to it than the ocean on the far side, so it feels a stronger pull. The solid Earth, sitting in the middle, feels an intermediate pull.

Now look at it from Earth's point of view. The near water is pulled away from the planet. The planet is pulled away from the far water. Both ends move outward relative to the centre. One cause, two bulges.

The correction

Tides are a stretch, not a lift

The useful mental swap is from pulling to stretching. Any object in a gravitational field that varies across its width gets stretched along the field direction and squeezed across it. Astronomers call the leftover a tidal force, and it is the difference between the pull here and the pull there — never the pull itself.

The same stretching tore apart comet Shoemaker–Levy 9 before it hit Jupiter, and it is what would happen to you near a black hole. Tides are not an ocean phenomenon. The ocean is just the part of Earth loose enough to show it.

Loose end

The sun raises tides too, at about 46% the strength of the moon's despite being overwhelmingly more massive — because tidal force falls off with the cube of distance, not the square. When sun and moon line up you get spring tides; at right angles, neap tides.
