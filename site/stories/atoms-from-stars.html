Where did the atoms in you come from?

The oxygen in your lungs, the calcium in your bones and the iron in your blood were each manufactured in a different kind of catastrophe, and they are all older than the Sun.

3 min read · you will come away knowing how to read your own composition as a history of the galaxy.

  1. The early universe made almost nothing

    The first few minutes after the Big Bang produced hydrogen, helium and a trace of lithium. That is the entire starting inventory. No carbon, no oxygen, no iron, nothing you are made of. Every heavier element had to be built afterwards, and building them takes stars.

  2. Everything heavier than helium was somewhere violent first.

  4. Iron is where fusion stops paying

    Fusion releases energy up to iron and consumes it beyond. When a massive star's core turns to iron, its energy supply ends in seconds and it collapses, then rebounds as a supernova that scatters everything it built across space. That debris seeds the next generation of stars. Our Sun is at least a third-generation star, which is why the solar system had heavy elements available to make planets and people out of.

  5. You are not made of star material as a metaphor. Every atom in you heavier than helium was assembled inside a star and released when that star died.

  7. How we know

    This is not a poetic claim; it is measured. Each element absorbs and emits light at its own specific wavelengths, so starlight carries a readable inventory of what a star contains. Older stars are demonstrably poorer in heavy elements than younger ones, exactly as the theory requires. And in 2017 a neutron star collision was observed directly, with heavy element production visible in the aftermath.

  8. Zoom out

    Your body is a record of galactic history. The proportions of the elements in you are a legible account of which stars lived, which died, and in what order.
