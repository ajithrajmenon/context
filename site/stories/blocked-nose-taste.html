Why does food taste of nothing with a cold?

Pinch your nose and eat a jelly sweet. You get sweet and sour and no idea which flavour it was meant to be. Let go and the name arrives instantly.

3 min read · you will come away knowing why illness and age flatten food, and what a cook does about it.

  1. Your tongue has a very short list

    Sweet, salty, sour, bitter and savoury. That is the entire repertoire of the tongue, and it has not grown since you were born.

    Everything else you think you are tasting — strawberry, coffee, roast lamb — is being detected somewhere else entirely.

  2. Most of what you call taste was never taste. It is smell, arriving from inside your mouth.

  4. It arrives by the back door

    When you chew, warm air carries volatile molecules up the back of your throat into the nasal cavity. This is retronasal smell, and it is the bulk of flavour.

    Your brain files it as taste because it happens while food is in your mouth. The location is a guess, and the guess is wrong.

  5. You have almost never tasted your food. You have smelled it, from the inside, and called that taste.

  7. What follows from it

    Smell fades with age and with many illnesses well before taste does. Food starts seeming bland and over-salted at once, which is the classic complaint about hospital meals.

    Cooks work around it with what the tongue can still reach: acid, salt and chilli heat. Adding more aroma to a person who cannot smell is wasted effort.

  8. Zoom out

    The sense you trust most at dinner is the one you are least aware of using. It is doing almost all the work and taking none of the credit.
