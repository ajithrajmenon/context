What lives at the bottom of the ocean?

Sunlight gives up after about two hundred metres. The sea keeps going for another eleven thousand — and every layer below that light has something living in it.

  1. Almost all of the ocean is dark

    The bright blue part you can picture is a thin skin on the top. Below two hundred metres, there is not enough light for a plant to live.

    That dark part is most of the living space on this planet, by a very long way.

  2. So they brought their own light

    Below about a kilometre, most of what you would see makes its own — for hunting, for hiding, and for finding each other in the dark.

  3. Going down

    1. 1

      0 to 200 m

      Sunlight. Nearly everything you can name lives up here, in about two per cent of the volume.

    2. 2

      200 to 1,000 m

      Twilight. Animals commute up at night to feed and sink back down by day — the largest migration on Earth, happening daily.

    3. 3

      1,000 to 6,000 m

      Total dark, near freezing. Food arrives as a slow drizzle of dead things from above.

    4. 4

      6,000 to 11,000 m

      The trenches. Pressure over a thousand times what you feel now. Still inhabited.

  4. More people have stood on the Moon than have been to the deepest point in the sea.

  6. And some of it never needed the Sun at all

    Around hot vents on the sea floor, whole communities run on chemicals from inside the Earth. No sunlight anywhere in the chain.

  7. Why that matters beyond Earth

    Before we found those vents, "life needs sunlight" was a safe assumption. It turned out to be a local habit rather than a rule.

    It is a large part of why the icy moons of Jupiter and Saturn, with dark oceans under their shells, are now serious places to go looking.

  8. Zoom out

    We have better maps of Mars than of our own sea floor. The largest habitat on this planet is the one we have looked at least.
