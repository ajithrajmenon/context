What is it like to fall into a black hole?

Nothing marks the event horizon. No wall, no flash, no sensation. For a large enough black hole you would cross the point of no return without noticing.

3 min read · you will come away knowing why two observers can honestly disagree about whether you ever arrived.

  1. The surface picture is wrong

    A black hole is usually pictured as an object with a surface. There is no surface. The horizon is a boundary defined entirely by where light can still get out, and it is not made of anything. Crossing it is not an impact; it is the moment a set of futures becomes unavailable.

  2. The horizon is not a place. It is a deadline.

  4. The two accounts cannot be reconciled, and both are right

    Someone watching from outside sees you slow, redden and dim as you approach, fading toward stillness and never quite arriving. You experience a fall of finite duration that ends. Neither observer is mistaken. Time is not a shared background clock, and there is no privileged account of when things happen.

  5. Inside the horizon, the centre is no longer a place you can steer away from. It is a moment in your future, like next Tuesday. That is what makes it inescapable.

  7. Where the physics runs out

    At the centre, the equations produce infinities, which is how a theory reports that it has been used outside its range. General relativity and quantum mechanics give incompatible answers there and we have no theory that covers both. What happens at the centre of a black hole is not a hard question; it is an unanswered one.

  8. Zoom out

    Black holes are where our two best theories of reality meet and contradict each other. That makes them less a curiosity than an address — the place we already know the current picture has to break.
