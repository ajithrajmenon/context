How does the universe end?

The universe is 13.8 billion years old, which feels ancient. Measured against how long it will exist, it has barely begun.

3 min read · you will come away knowing what the far future looks like, and where we sit in it.

  1. Star formation has already peaked

    The rate at which the universe makes new stars reached its maximum around ten billion years ago and has been falling since. Most of the stars that will ever exist have already formed. The visible, bright, star-filled cosmos is not the normal state of things; it is a brief opening chapter.

  2. We arrived absurdly early, while the lights were still on.

  4. The end is not a bang

    Given current measurements, the most likely ending is not a collapse or an explosion but a slow flattening. Expansion continues, galaxies drift out of contact, stars exhaust their fuel, remnants cool, and eventually even black holes evaporate. What remains is a thin, cold, uniform nothing in which no process can run because there are no differences left to exploit. Not destruction — exhaustion.

  5. The universe does not end in an event. It runs down until there are no differences left, and a universe with no differences has nothing left to happen in it.

  7. Which makes now unusual

    In a hundred billion years, distant galaxies will have receded beyond visibility. Any astronomers then will see one galaxy in an empty static sky and have no way to discover expansion, no cosmic background radiation to measure, and no evidence the universe had a beginning. The information we used to work all this out is finite and it is leaving.

  8. Zoom out

    We are not living at the end of the universe. We are living at the very beginning, in the narrow window when it is still possible to find out what kind of universe it is. That is a strange piece of luck to have been handed.
