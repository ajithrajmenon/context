Life

Is a virus alive?

A virus sits at the exact edge of the definition of life, and biology has never fully agreed which side it falls on. That is not a gap in our knowledge — it is a problem with the definition.

  1. A virus is instructions in a box

    Strip one down and you find a short set of genetic instructions wrapped in a protein shell. Sometimes a fatty coat over that.

    That is the entire organism. No mouth, no engine, no way of making anything. Left alone, it does nothing at all — indefinitely.

  2. It only becomes anything when it finds a cell

    The shell docks, the instructions go in, and the cell starts building copies of the thing that infected it.

  3. The usual tests for being alive

    1. 1

      Uses energy

      A virus does not. It has no metabolism of any kind. FAILS.

    2. 2

      Grows

      It never grows. It is assembled at full size. FAILS.

    3. 3

      Reproduces

      Only by making a cell do it. On its own, never. HALF.

    4. 4

      Evolves

      Spectacularly well. This is why flu jabs are yearly. PASSES.

  4. A virus is not alive, and not dead. It is a set of instructions waiting for a machine that can read them.

  6. There are more of them than anything else

    Around ten to the thirty-first, by rough count. Laid end to end they would reach further than the nearest galaxies. Most infect bacteria, not you.

  7. And some of them are in you permanently

    About eight per cent of your DNA came from viruses that infected your ancestors and never left.

    One of those leftover genes is essential for building a placenta. Mammals may owe live birth to an ancient infection.

  8. Zoom out

    Nature does not care about our categories. We drew a line called "alive", and viruses sat down exactly on top of it and have not moved since.
