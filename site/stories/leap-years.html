Why do we have leap years?

Two natural clocks — the Earth turning and the Earth orbiting — run at rates with no common factor. Every calendar in history is an attempt to paper over that.

3 min read · you will come away knowing why 2100 will not be a leap year, and what happens if you forget.

  1. The mismatch is small and relentless

    One orbit takes about 365.2422 days. Round it to 365 and you lose almost a quarter of a day a year. That sounds harmless. It is six hours annually, a full day every four years, and about twenty-four days per century.

  2. The year does not divide evenly by the day. Every calendar is an accumulating apology.

  4. The fix needed a fix

    Adding a day every four years overcorrects, because the leftover is 0.2422 rather than 0.25. That small excess accumulated until the calendar had drifted ten days by the sixteenth century and Easter was visibly wrong. The correction was to skip the leap day in years divisible by 100 — but keep it in years divisible by 400. So 1900 was not a leap year, 2000 was, and 2100 will not be.

  5. The rule looks arbitrary because it is not a rule about time. It is a running repayment schedule for a debt that can never be cleared exactly.

  7. And it still is not finished

    The Earth's rotation is not perfectly steady either — tides slow it very gradually, and it wobbles unpredictably year to year. Leap seconds were introduced to cope, caused enough trouble for computer systems that a decision was taken to phase them out by 2035, and the underlying mismatch simply continues without them.

  8. Zoom out

    We treat the calendar as a fact about the world. It is a negotiated settlement between two clocks that do not agree, renegotiated roughly once a millennium.
