Origins

Everything you are was made inside a star

The early universe could build almost nothing. Every atom in you heavier than helium had to be manufactured somewhere violent, and then thrown back out.

The universe began with a very short shopping list

For the first few minutes there was hydrogen, helium, and a trace of lithium. That is the entire inventory. No carbon, no oxygen, no iron, nothing to make a rock out of, let alone a person.

Everything else had to be assembled later, one nucleus at a time, in the only places hot and heavy enough to do it.

Stars are furnaces that eventually fail

A star spends its life fusing light elements into heavier ones. Hydrogen into helium, then helium into carbon and oxygen, each stage hotter and faster than the last.

Then it hits iron. Fusing iron costs energy instead of releasing it, so the furnace stalls, the core collapses, and the star tears itself apart — scattering everything it ever made.

Every atom in you heavier than helium was somewhere else violent first.

And some of it needed something even rarer

Supernovae cannot account for all of it. The heaviest elements — gold, platinum, the iodine your thyroid runs on — appear to come mostly from neutron stars colliding.

We watched that happen for the first time in 2017. Two dead stellar cores spiralled together and threw off a cloud containing, by some estimates, several Earth masses of gold.

Zoom out

This is not a metaphor and it is not a nice way of putting things. The iron moving oxygen around your body this second was forged in a collapsing star and flung across the galaxy before the Sun had formed. It ended up here.
