Why does the other queue always move faster?

This feels like a persecution complex and turns out to be straightforward arithmetic.

3 min read · you will come away knowing why the single-line system exists, and what it fixes.

  1. It is not confirmation bias

    The standard explanation is that you only remember the times you lost. That is real, and it is not the main effect. Consider three queues. Delays are random, so on average one queue is slowest. The chance you are in it is one in three, and the chance one of the other two is faster than yours is two in three. You are genuinely outpaced most of the time.

  2. Usually it does. And the reason is not luck.

  4. And queues make their own weather

    Queues do not just have random delays; they generate them. One person with a problem stalls an entire line, and that line then attracts nobody new while everyone already in it waits. Meanwhile people join the shortest line, which lengthens it, so lines constantly overshoot and correct. Switching lanes makes it worse — you pay the cost of joining the back of a new queue, and you do it just as your old one clears.

  5. You are usually in the slower queue because in any set of queues, most people are. Somebody has to be, and there are more of them than there are of you.

  7. Which is the real finding

    Research on queueing consistently shows people are less bothered by long waits than by unexplained or unfair ones. Occupied waits feel shorter than unoccupied ones, and a stated wait time feels shorter than an unknown one. That is why theme parks post estimates and deliberately overstate them slightly, and why lifts have mirrors.

  8. Zoom out

    Most of what looks like a speed problem is a fairness problem wearing a clock. Fixing the feeling is often cheaper and more effective than fixing the wait.
