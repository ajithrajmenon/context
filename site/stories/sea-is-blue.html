Why is the sea blue?

The reflection explanation is repeated everywhere and is mostly wrong. Water is blue on its own account, and you can demonstrate it indoors.

3 min read · you will come away knowing how to read depth, life and sediment off the colour of water.

  1. Test the usual answer

    If the sea were blue because the sky is, a grey overcast sea would be grey, and it is not — it stays blue-green. Deep water in an indoor pool under artificial light is blue. Photographs taken looking straight down, where no sky can reflect, are blue. Reflection contributes at shallow angles and is not the cause.

  2. Not the sky's reflection. Water is genuinely, faintly blue.

  4. Water absorbs red light

    The water molecule vibrates in a way that absorbs light at the red end of the spectrum, weakly but persistently. Over a few metres, red is removed; over tens of metres, orange and yellow follow. What is left to scatter back to your eye is blue. In a glass you see nothing, because the effect is far too weak over ten centimetres. Look through several metres and it is unmistakable.

  5. The sea is blue for the same reason a forest is green: it absorbs everything else. Blue is what survives the journey.

  7. Which is why colour is a measurement

    Ocean colour is not decoration; it is data. Satellites read the green signal to estimate how much phytoplankton is growing across the whole planet, week by week. That single measurement underpins much of what we know about carbon uptake by the ocean and about fisheries.

  8. Zoom out

    The bluest sea in the holiday photograph is the emptiest one. Once you can read the colour, the picture is saying something different from what it was taken to say.
