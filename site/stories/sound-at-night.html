Why does sound travel further at night?

A train you never notice by day is clearly audible at two in the morning. Part of that is the quiet. Most of it is not.

3 min read · you will come away knowing how to read the weather off what you can hear.

  1. The obvious explanation is only half of it

    Background noise does fall at night, so faint sounds clear the threshold. But that cannot explain the whole effect: people report hearing specific distant sources — a motorway, a church bell, a factory — with a clarity that daytime quiet in remote places does not reproduce.

  2. Not because it is quieter. The air itself is bending sound back down to you.

  4. Sound bends toward the slow side

    Sound travels faster in warmer air. When the ground is hot, the lower part of a wavefront outruns the upper part and the whole wave tips upward and escapes over your head. After sunset the ground radiates heat away and the layer just above it becomes the coldest, so the bottom of the wave is now the slowest, the wave tips downward, and sound that would have left the area is steered back to the ground and can travel far beyond its usual range.

  5. It is not the silence. The night air is acting as a lens, and it is pointed at you.

  7. The same physics, elsewhere

    This is refraction, and it is the reason a straw looks bent in water and the reason distant objects appear to float above a hot road. The ocean has a version too: a depth where sound is slowest traps it in a channel, and whale calls in that layer travel for hundreds of kilometres.

  8. Zoom out

    Once you know that waves bend toward whatever slows them, a set of unrelated everyday oddities turn out to be the same fact seen from different places.
