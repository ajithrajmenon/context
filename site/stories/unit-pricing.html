Is the big box actually cheaper?

Buying the larger size is the most widely believed piece of household economics there is, and it is wrong often enough to cost you real money.

3 min read · you will come away knowing the one number to look at, and the two places shops hide it.

  1. The assumption is doing the work

    Everyone knows bigger is cheaper per unit, so almost nobody checks. Audits of supermarket shelves routinely find between one in five and one in four products where the larger pack costs more per unit than the smaller — sometimes considerably more.

  2. Often. Not always. And the label is designed so that you cannot tell at a glance.

  4. It is a pricing decision, not a cost

    The larger pack is not priced from what it costs to make. It is priced from what you will pay, and what you will pay is driven by the belief that big means cheap. The unit price is legally required on the label in many places precisely because of this — and it is printed small, and shops use different units for competing products on the same shelf, so grams and hundred-grams sit side by side and cannot be compared without arithmetic.

  5. The big box is not a discount. It is a bet that you will not do the division, and it is a bet that pays.

  7. What to actually do

    Read the unit price, not the shelf price, and check the two labels are in the same units. For anything perishable, price what you will eat rather than what you will buy. That is the whole method, it takes a few seconds, and it beats every loyalty scheme you have.

  8. Zoom out

    This one is small and it compounds. A few percent on everything you buy, every week, for a lifetime, is a large number arriving in instalments too small to notice.
