What if antibiotics stopped working?

Ask what a world without working antibiotics looks like and most people picture dying of pneumonia. The real loss is broader and stranger: almost all of modern medicine quietly assumes infection can be handled.

3 min read · you will come away knowing what resistance actually costs, and the one thing an individual controls.

  1. Antibiotics are infrastructure, not a treatment

    A hip replacement, a caesarean, a bowel resection, chemotherapy, an organ transplant, intensive care — every one of these opens the body or suppresses immunity, and every one is safe only because infection afterwards is treatable. Withdraw that and the procedures do not become harder. They become bad bets.

  2. The problem is not untreatable infections. It is that routine surgery becomes a gamble.

  4. Resistance is evolution running on a timescale you can watch

    Expose a bacterial population to a drug and the few that survive are the ones that reproduce. Bacteria divide in minutes and swap resistance genes directly between individuals, including across species, on small loops of DNA. That is why resistance to a new drug typically appears within a few years of its introduction — and why the pipeline matters: most classes in use were discovered before 1970, and the economics of developing a drug you must then use as little as possible are terrible.

  5. Resistance is not a failure of the drugs. It is the predictable result of using them, which means the question was never whether it happens but how fast we let it.

  7. It is a slow emergency, already underway

    Resistant infections are associated with well over a million deaths a year worldwide and contribute to several million more. That is not a forecast. What the forecasts add is direction: the trend is upward, and the countries with the least access to functioning antibiotics carry most of the burden — resistance and simple unavailability arriving as the same problem.

  8. Zoom out

    This is what an emergency looks like when it moves slowly enough: no moment to point at, and a steady withdrawal of things we had stopped thinking of as achievements at all.
