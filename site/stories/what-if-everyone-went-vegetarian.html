What if everyone stopped eating meat?

The argument is usually had about carbon. The larger and less discussed number is how much of the planet's surface livestock occupies.

3 min read · you will come away knowing which food choices actually move the numbers, and which barely register.

  1. Most farmland does not feed people directly

    Agriculture uses roughly half the world's habitable land, and livestock accounts for around 77% of that while supplying about 18% of calories and 37% of protein. Whatever you conclude from it, that ratio is the central fact of the food system and it is not close.

  2. The big change is land, not emissions. About three quarters of farmland would be freed.

  4. Freed land is the lever, not the meal

    Ending livestock farming would cut food-system emissions by something like two thirds. But the larger effect is that an area roughly the size of the Americas stops being farmed, and if it returns to forest and grassland it absorbs carbon for decades — a one-off drawdown large enough to matter on its own. Emissions reduction is a tap; land return is a sponge, and the sponge has been the underrated half.

  5. The strongest argument for eating less meat is not what it stops putting into the air. It is what it lets grow back.

  7. Which makes the useful advice unusually specific

    Cutting beef and lamb does most of the work; going from chicken to beans does comparatively little. Transport is a small share for nearly everything, so buying local matters far less than people assume, and what you eat matters far more than how far it travelled. And some grazing land genuinely cannot grow crops, so a world with no livestock at all is not the same as the optimal one.

  8. Zoom out

    Almost nobody will stop eating meat. Which is why it matters that the effect is so concentrated: most of the available gain comes from one or two items, not from the whole diet.
