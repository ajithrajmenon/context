What if the internet went down for a week?

The obvious losses are the visible ones. The ones that would actually change your week are systems you never interact with directly.

3 min read · you will come away knowing which everyday systems have no offline fallback left.

  1. Communication is the resilient part

    Phone networks and broadcast radio run on separate infrastructure and would largely survive. People would talk, and news would travel. Communication is the layer with the most redundancy, because it is the oldest.

  2. The problem is not that you cannot message anyone. It is that the shops cannot restock.

  4. Modern supply is a conversation, not a stockpile

    A supermarket holds a few days of stock because it does not need more: tills report sales, systems reorder automatically, lorries are routed overnight. That efficiency is bought by removing the buffer — warehouses, back rooms, spare capacity. Cut the conversation and the buffer is not there to fall back on, which is why a short outage empties shelves that a long one in 1980 would not have.

  5. The internet is not mostly a communication network any more. It is the coordination layer for the physical movement of goods, and that layer replaced the stockpiles we used to keep instead.

  7. Which is a general lesson about efficiency

    Every buffer removed is a real saving, and also the removal of something that used to absorb shocks. That trade is usually invisible, because the saving shows up every year and the shock shows up once. It is the same trade behind fragile power grids, single-supplier medicines and hospitals run at full occupancy.

  8. Zoom out

    Resilience looks like waste right up until the morning you need it. That is the hardest thing about paying for it.
